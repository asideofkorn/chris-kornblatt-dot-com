01 · Problem

The problem a spectator actually has

An IRONMAN is a 2.4-mile swim, a 112-mile bike and a 26.2-mile run, and most athletes are out there for 11 to 16 hours. A spectator gets a few chances to see their athlete: a few seconds each, spread across a course you can't walk.

The official app shows an estimated dot on a map. That answers "where is he?" But it doesn't answer what a spectator needs to decide:

  • Where should I stand, and when will he pass it?
  • When do I have to leave to get there in time?
  • How do I get there on foot, from where I am now?
Miss him at a spot and the next chance can be hours away. The cost of a wrong estimate is asymmetric: early is boring, late is a lost moment.

Who it's for

One spectator (me), plus friends I could send the link to. All on phones, often one-handed, outdoors in bright sun, with spotty signal near 3,000 athletes' worth of crowds. That ruled out anything that needed an account, an install, or an API key, and pushed every screen to answer one question at a glance.

02 · Roadmap

Priorities set by the race clock

The roadmap ordered itself by when each need would hit. Anything needed before Bradley got in the water had to ship first. Everything else could be built in the long waits between sightings. All times below are Pacific, on race day.

P0Before the start: never miss him

  1. 2:38 am First request: a product brief before any code
  2. 3:08 am Tracker live on this site: a prediction from start time and paces, plus spots to see him with ETAs
  3. 3:34 am Map moved to OpenStreetMap tiles, after the first tile service wanted a key
  4. 4:21 am Bradley's official timing splits pulled live, the real course drawn, and the whole field's times used to sharpen estimates
  5. 4:36 am Map filters by sport, with arrows showing direction of travel
  6. 4:52 am Every spot gets a Map button and walking directions in Google Maps, Apple Maps or Waze
  7. 4:59 am Map locks until tapped, so scrolling the page doesn't drag it, and gets a full-screen mode
  8. 5:40 am Predictions re-based on the swim, bike and run targets Bradley shared that morning, with forecast vs actual kept side by side

P1Race morning: trust the estimate, and don't lose the data

  1. 6:53 am IRONMAN app's estimated position shown next to ours, with an Ours / IRONMAN / Both toggle
  2. 7:07 am Every estimate saved over time for a post-race replay, with a download
  3. 7:11 am A backup recorder running in the cloud, so the race record survives even if my phone dies
  4. 7:20 am Live place, overall and in his age group, at his latest timing mat
  5. 7:47 am Bradley starts. Start time and race clock shown on the Now card
  6. 7:54 am Before the app publishes its own estimate, we approximate it from the field, labeled as an approximation

P2While he swims and rides: understand the race

  1. 7:32 am Race timetable: every athlete as one line (a Marey chart), with where people dropped out
  2. 7:46 am Transition times across the field
  3. 8:03 am Anonymized field data refreshed hourly and redeployed automatically
  4. 8:08 am Bradley's place through the day
  5. 8:16 am Course crowding: how many athletes were on each stretch at each time
  6. 8:22 am Wind on the bike, with weather saved through the day
  7. 8:31 am River flow during the swim saved alongside it

P2 work overlaps the end of P1 on purpose: once the core loop worked, the charts were built in the gaps while P1 fixes came from watching the live page.

03 · Product

What shipped

Five tabs, with the most urgent answer first. Now opens by default: where he probably is, his finish range, and every spot with when he'll pass and when to leave. Map puts our estimate, the official app's estimate and your own location on the real course. Plan keeps the original forecast next to actual times. Log holds the replay data. Charts covers the whole race.

04 · Judgment

Decisions and trade-offs

Ranges, not false precision

Every ETA is a window from fast to slow. For a spectator, the early edge is what matters, so leave-by times are computed from it.

Be a good guest on someone else's API

The timing feed is public but not ours. Field pulls were cut to about one request a second, the public charts refresh hourly, and full pulls run at four checkpoints.

Privacy by default

The public charts cover about 3,000 athletes with no names, bibs or ages; only Bradley is identified. The raw data stays in a private repository.

Label the guesses

When the official estimate isn't published yet, we approximate it and say so on the map. Simulated test data was never published as real.

No keys, no installs

OpenStreetMap tiles and a static page on GitHub Pages. Nothing to sign up for, and nothing to break at 6 am.

Redundancy where loss is permanent

Race-day data can't be re-collected, so it's recorded twice: on the phone and in the cloud.

A trade-off we saw live

During the swim, our approximation of the official estimate ran ahead of the real app. The only athletes it could learn from were the fastest swimmers, who were already out of the water, so it skewed fast. Since the official estimate replaces it at the first mat, I kept the "approximate" label and didn't change the model mid-race.

05 · Process

How it was built, and what's next

I worked as product owner with Claude Code as the engineer, in a cloud session, mostly from my phone. I set the problem, the priorities and the constraints, reviewed screenshots, and caught issues from real use: a key-gated map, an athlete list that quietly stopped short of the full field, and a confirm button nobody needed once official times were flowing. Each fix went live on this site within minutes.

The first thing written was a product brief, not code. It defined the job, the users and the failure that mattered most: missing him. It meant the 5 am work was the right work.

What's next

  • Prediction accuracy: our estimates vs the official app's vs what actually happened, measured after he finishes and the overnight data pull lands.
  • A full-race replay built from the recorded estimates.
  • The remaining charts once the field finishes.
